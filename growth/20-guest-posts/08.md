# How to Run a 2-Week Software Trial Like an Analyst: A 12-Task Checklist

The two-week trial is the most expensive free thing in software buying. It costs your team two weeks of time, and most of that time is wasted, because trials are run like sandboxes, not experiments. Users poke around, a salesperson books a follow-up demo, and at the end of day fourteen you know the product's color scheme but nothing about its behavior under your data, your users, and your scale.

Analysts run trials as protocols: a fixed task list, run in a fixed order, with a pass/fail/flag verdict recorded for each item. This checklist is exactly that protocol — twelve tasks, six in week one, six in week two. Run every task, record every result, and you will know more about the product on day fourteen than most teams know after a quarter.

## Before You Start: Define the Core Workflow

Pick the single workflow the tool must handle daily — the one that, if it broke, would ruin your team's week. For a help desk, that is a ticket from inbox to resolution with one internal note. For CRM, quote to contract to invoice. Write the workflow as a sequence of five to seven concrete steps, and prepare a test dataset of real records (stripped of personal data). The entire trial hangs on this workflow, so choose it carefully: the workflow that sells you on the product is the workflow that will run in production.

## Week One: Functionality Under Real Load

**Task 1: Core workflow test.** Run your workflow end to end, five times, with the test data. Log every step that takes a detour, every extra click, every manual workaround — and time each run. A workflow that takes nine minutes the first time and four minutes the fifth time is learning-friendly; a workflow that takes the same nine minutes every time is a workflow that will still take nine minutes in month nine. A workflow that needs a workaround on day three will need it in month twelve.

**Task 2: Real data import.** Import 10,000 real records — realistic volume, realistic messiness, duplicate names, missing fields. Time the import, note what gets mangled, and check whether the mapping step is something a business user can do, not a job that requires a consultant. Also test the re-import: your team will re-import or sync data weekly, so a one-time hero import with a manual cleanup ritual is a recurring cost, not a setup fee. Products that shine on clean demo data often break on real data.

**Task 3: Non-technical user test.** Hand the product to the least technical person in your office, with no training and thirty minutes. Watch where they get stuck — not where you think they should get stuck. If the least technical user cannot complete the core workflow, the product's real cost includes a permanent training tax.

**Task 4: Permissions test.** Create four roles: admin, editor, viewer, and external collaborator. Log in as each one and verify exactly what they can see and change. Check the two dangerous spots: whether external users can see internal records, and whether the admin can undo anything.

**Task 5: Search at scale.** After the import, search across all 10,000 records with the filters your team actually uses. Test saved searches, combined filters, and the slowest edge case you can invent — a three-level filter with a date range and a text search across attachments, say. Search performance is a proxy for database design: if search crawls at 10,000 records, imagine it at 500,000, and check whether the product indexes attachments at all, because "searchable" frequently means "searchable titles only."

**Task 6: Reporting.** Rebuild your three most important reports from scratch in the product. Check calculated fields, date ranges, grouping, and whether the report can be exported or scheduled. Reports are where vendors overpromise most, so verify every formula with a known number.

## Week Two: The Untold Tests

**Task 7: Support ticket test.** File a real support ticket with a real, time-consuming problem. Measure first response time and resolution quality against the response time in their SLA. Support in the trial is a preview of support in year two — vendors who ghost you during the sales trial will not improve after you pay.

**Task 8: Mobile test.** Complete the core workflow on mobile, on the oldest phone in the office, over a mediocre connection. Check what breaks, what refuses to load, and whether anything requires the desktop. If your team works from the field, this is not a nice-to-have; it is the product.

**Task 9: Data export.** Export everything — records, attachments, comments, history — and verify completeness, not just success. Open the files, count the records, compare against the source. The export is your contract with your future self; run Task 9 before you love the product, so the exit decision stays yours.

**Task 10: Changelog review.** Read the last six months of changelog entries. Count how many are new features versus breaking changes, and note the cadence of releases. A product that ships weekly but breaks integrations monthly has a different risk profile than a product that ships quarterly and stays stable.

**Task 11: API limits.** Read the API documentation and test the specifics: rate limits, webhook delivery, record limits on bulk calls. If your integration plan depends on the API, verify those numbers in the trial sandbox — the API docs are marketing material until tested.

**Task 12: Itemized quote.** Ask for a quote with every line item written out: seats, storage, support tier, overages, setup fee, contract length, and annual lock-in. If any line item cannot be explained in plain terms, that is where the surprise will come from in year two. Also ask for the renewal notice: what does the same package cost after the introductory pricing expires, and what written notice is required to leave at the end of the term?

## How to Grade the Results

Grade each task pass, fail, or flag. Pass means the task completed as specified. Fail means it was impossible or broken. Flag means it worked with friction — note the friction verbatim, because "worked but I had to export to CSV first" is a very different flag from "worked but the button is on the second screen." Two fails is grounds for rejection. Three flags inside the core workflow is grounds for rejection. Everything else is trade-off, and trade-offs go to the weighted scorecard, not the gut.

Do the grading the day each task completes, not on day fourteen — memories smooth over friction, and the log must stay raw. This pass/fail/flag discipline is the same one that underpins [PilotStack's published methodology](https://www.pilotstack.online/methodology) — fixed criteria applied to every tool so the results are comparable — and it works because it removes the salesman from the verdict.

## Conclusion

A two-week trial run on this checklist tells you more than three months of demos: import behavior at realistic volume, search performance at scale, support quality under real questions, export freedom, and the true itemized cost. By day fourteen, you will know whether the product survives contact with your reality — and the trial log, not the demo, is what your procurement committee will sign. Run the twelve tasks, keep the verdicts, and the decision makes itself.
