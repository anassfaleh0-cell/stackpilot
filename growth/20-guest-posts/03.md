# 7 Signs Your Software Stack Needs an Audit (and How to Run One in a Day)

Most companies have no idea what they're paying for software. Not a vague "we should look at this someday" gap — a real, quantified ignorance: the average mid-size business runs 30 to 100 distinct SaaS applications, and most finance teams can name fewer than half of them. The rest sit in a spreadsheet of recurring invoices nobody has opened in two years, or on a company card where they were purchased as "one-time" tools that turned out to be annual subscriptions.

The fix isn't a six-month consulting engagement. Four hours of data gathering and one working day of analysis are enough to find the 10–20% of your stack budget that's quietly burning. Here are the seven signs that you need one, and a day-long agenda to run it.

## Sign 1: Duplicate Tools Doing the Same Job

The classic: three project management tools because three teams each chose their own, or two video conferencing platforms because the marketing team liked one brand while engineering signed up for the other. Duplication is rarely a judgment failure — it's a purchasing failure, because nobody had a list of what was already approved.

The most expensive duplicate category is the one nobody notices: data storage and automation layers. Two file-sync tools, three team-chat platforms, or four A/B testing tools all run silently, and each carries its own seat minimum, integration, and admin burden.

The audit move: categorize every application by function, count the duplicates, and pick a single tool per category. Consolidating two tools in one category typically recovers 30–50% of that category's spend, and the productivity gain of "one place to look" exceeds the dollar savings.

## Sign 2: Ghost Seats

Ghost seats are licenses that are paid but not used — an employee who left in March, a department that never onboarded its allocation, or minimum seats you're contractually paying for whether you use them or not. Audit data across thousands of companies consistently shows 25–40% of purchased seats unused in a typical organization.

The audit move: pull a login-activity report from every platform and compare it to the invoice. Any license with zero logins in 90 days is a ghost. Before you cut, sanity-check — seasonal users, contractors, and the executive who logs in quarterly but controls your access may all be legitimate. But the default for a zero-login seat is cancellation or downgrade to the minimum tier.

## Sign 3: Usage Cliffs

A usage cliff is where adoption collapses — the tool was deployed with fanfare, 60% of the team used it in month one, and by month six that number is 12%. The subscription continues at full price while the value vanishes. Usage cliffs are the most common failure pattern in SaaS, and they're expensive precisely because they're invisible: no alert fires when a tool stops being used.

The audit move: for every application, chart monthly active users against seats paid. Then ask whether the cliff is fixable or structural. Fixable means the tool does its job but onboarding was bad — a champion, training, and a workflow refresh can recover it. Structural means the tool solves a problem that no longer exists, or duplicates another tool — in which case the cliff is a fact, and the subscription is a donation.

## Sign 4: Shadow IT

Shadow IT is software bought outside the procurement process — typically via a company credit card or a personal account holding work data. It's not a discipline problem; it's a signal that the approved stack doesn't meet a real need. When marketing uses a free file-transfer tool with client data because the approved one caps at 25 MB, the shadow tool isn't the problem — the cap is.

The audit move: match credit card statements against the approved-vendor list, and ask department heads which unofficial tools their teams use daily. Then apply the two-outcome rule: either bring the shadow tool into the approved stack with a proper contract, or fix the approved tool so the need disappears. An audit that cancels shadow tools without fixing the underlying gap will have them back within two quarters.

## Sign 5: Renewal Dates You Can't Find

Every subscription has a renewal date, and vendors design the notification so you miss the negotiation window. The median behavior is telling: a majority of mid-market renewals happen without any competitive quote, and vendors know that — which is why their renewal pricing routinely exceeds the new-customer price by 10–20% for identical terms.

The audit move: build a renewal calendar — every contract, renewal date, notice window (usually 30–60 days), and current price. Flag anything that auto-renews within 60 days. You don't need to renegotiate everything; you need to negotiate the ones with notice windows coming up, and to build the calendar so renewals become decisions rather than defaults. If you want a reference for how rigorous, rubric-based evaluation looks when applied at scale — the kind of structure that turns audit findings into defensible buying decisions — [PilotStack's](https://www.pilotstack.online/methodology) published methodology is a good model to borrow from.

## Sign 6: Stack Sprawl Per Department

Even without duplicates, stacks accumulate like sediment — each year adds a tool, and nobody removes one. The audit often finds that a single department carries 15–20 subscriptions, several of which serve a single employee's workflow or were added to satisfy one project that ended in 2023.

The audit move: rank each application by two factors — number of users and criticality. The bottom-left quadrant (few users, low criticality) is your first cut list. The top-right quadrant is your retention list. Everything in between gets a conversation. The discipline that prevents sprawl going forward is a simple one: no new application gets purchased without a named owner and a date to review it in six months.

## How to Run the Audit in One Day (and What It Shouldn't Do)

The seventh sign — the one that covers all the others — is that nobody can answer "what do we spend on software per year?" within 10%. If the number lives in someone's head, or in an accountant's folder of invoices, you're not managing the stack; you're discovering it. The audit day is what produces the single source of truth, and it fits in eight working hours with two people maximum:

**Hours 1–2: Gather the data.** Export bank and credit card statements for the last 12 months, filter recurring software charges, and pull login-activity reports from your ten biggest platforms. One person owns this; a template spreadsheet saves the next two hours.

**Hour 3: Build the master list.** Every application, annual cost, category, and owner. If you find 60 apps and can only name the purpose of 40, that's your finding of the day.

**Hour 4: Duplicates and shadow IT.** Match tools by function, and match card charges against the approved list.

**Hour 5: Usage.** Login counts against seats paid. Mark ghosts and cliffs.

**Hour 6: Renewals.** Renewal dates, notice windows, and any auto-renewal within 60 days.

**Hours 7–8: Prioritize.** Score every action as quick win (under an hour to execute, clear savings), strategic (needs negotiation or consolidation planning), or no-go (tool is critical, leave it). A typical audit produces 10–15 quick wins representing 10–20% of stack spend.

A one-day audit finds the waste; it doesn't fix it. Don't cancel anything during the audit itself except the most obvious ghosts — cancellations without a consolidation plan create the shadow IT gap that immediately re-fills. Instead, end the day with a prioritized list, owners assigned, and the renewal calendar built, then execute the quick wins within a month. And keep the discipline alive with a quarterly review: one hour per quarter with the master list, the renewal calendar, and a standing rule that any new tool requires a named owner and a review date.

**Actionable takeaways:**

1. Duplicates are the biggest single recovery: consolidate one category at a time, and expect 30–50% savings per category.
2. Kill ghost seats with a 90-day zero-login rule, sanity-checked against legitimate users.
3. Classify usage cliffs as fixable or structural before spending a dollar on re-training.
4. Fix the need that caused the shadow tool, or the shadow tool will return.
5. Build a renewal calendar with notice windows; negotiate before the window closes, not after.
6. Rank every app by users × criticality; cut the bottom-left quadrant.
7. End the day with a master spreadsheet and a quarterly review habit, not a pile of cancellations.
