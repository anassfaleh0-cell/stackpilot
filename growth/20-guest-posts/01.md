# How to Build a Software Evaluation Scorecard (Weighted Decision Matrix)

Most software purchases fail before the vendor demo ever happens — not because the wrong tool wins, but because nobody defined what "right" means. A weighted decision matrix fixes that: a spreadsheet discipline that forces every stakeholder to state what they value before seeing anything, then scores every candidate against the same yardstick. Done properly, it turns a three-month tug-of-war into a one-page, CFO-ready decision.

A pro-and-con list isn't a scorecard. Without weights, every factor counts equally, so a tool's flashy interface cancels out its broken API, and lists invite recency bias — the demo you watched last Tuesday dominates the notes from three weeks ago. A weighted matrix fixes both problems: weights force prioritization (if everything is important, nothing is), and scores captured against a fixed rubric mean a vendor's glossy story can't retroactively inflate a mediocre score. The matrix also gives you a paper trail: when the sales rep asks why you chose another vendor, or your team second-guesses the decision six months later, you point to the scored rows, not your memory.

## Step 1: Assemble the Scoring Team First

Decide who has a vote before you look at any product. The right group is usually five to eight people spanning the functions the tool will touch: a daily power user, the occasional user who represents the majority, an administrator, IT or security, and one budget owner with veto power.

Two rules keep it honest. Everyone scores independently — no open discussion until the sheet is filled, or the loudest voice wins. And the budget owner gets a vote but not extra points; finance approval is a separate gate. Mixing affordability into feature scores is how teams end up buying a tool nobody uses because it was cheap.

## Step 2: Define Criteria, Not Features

This is the step most teams rush. Write criteria as outcomes, not feature checklists. "Real-time editing" is a feature; "remote team can co-edit documents without version conflicts" is a criterion. Features get scored against marketing claims; outcomes get scored against your actual workflow.

A practical method: collect each stakeholder's top three frustrations with the current system and turn them into criteria. If support is drowning in manual ticket routing, "auto-route tickets by topic and priority" becomes a criterion.

Keep the list between six and ten criteria. Beyond ten, weights get split so thinly that nothing separates the candidates — ten is the ceiling for a matrix a room of people can agree on in one sitting.

## Step 3: Assign Weights That Add Up to 100

Each criterion gets a weight, and weights must sum to 100. Force everyone to allocate from the same 100 points rather than voting criterion by criterion; that friction is where real priorities surface. You'll discover, for instance, that sales believes "integration with the CRM" is worth 25 points while IT thinks it's worth 5 — and that argument must happen before vendors arrive, not after.

A realistic weight spread for a mid-size team buying a project management tool might look like this:

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Ease of adoption for non-technical staff | 20 |
| Reporting depth for leadership | 15 |
| Integration with existing CRM/email | 15 |
| Vendor support responsiveness | 12 |
| Data export and portability | 12 |
| Security and compliance posture | 10 |
| Mobile experience for field staff | 8 |
| AI-assisted planning features | 8 |

Two sanity checks: any criterion under 5% weight is probably noise, and any criterion over 30% makes the matrix collapse into a one-variable decision, which defeats the purpose.

## Step 4: Score on a Fixed 1–5 Scale with Written Anchors

The most common mistake in scoring is letting each person invent their own scale. Define it once, with written anchors, at the top of the spreadsheet:

- 5 = Exceeds the requirement; solves it better than any alternative
- 4 = Fully meets the requirement out of the box
- 3 = Meets it with configuration or minor workarounds
- 2 = Partially meets it; significant manual work remains
- 1 = Does not meet it in any practical form

Anchors compress the range. Without them, one person's "decent" is another's "great," and scores compare apples to oranges; with them, a 3 means the same thing to every rater.

## Step 5: Score the Current System Too

Run your existing tool through the same matrix before you score any candidate. This is the step that saves the most money, because it reveals whether the pain is real or imagined. If your current system scores 68/100 and the leading candidate scores 71/100 with six weeks of migration work attached, the switch is a bad trade. If the current system scores 52 and the candidate scores 74, the spreadsheet has just built the business case you'll need to get budget approval.

It also calibrates your scores: people naturally inflate unfamiliar tools, so scoring the incumbent first establishes a baseline that makes candidate scores trustworthy.

## A Worked Example: The Full Matrix

Here's a realistic example: a 15-person consultancy choosing between three project management platforms, scores averaged from six independent raters.

| Criterion | Weight | Vendor A | Vendor B | Vendor C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ease of adoption | 20 | 4 (80) | 3 (60) | 5 (100) |
| Reporting depth | 15 | 5 (75) | 3 (45) | 2 (30) |
| CRM/email integration | 15 | 4 (60) | 5 (75) | 2 (30) |
| Support responsiveness | 12 | 3 (36) | 4 (48) | 3 (36) |
| Data portability | 12 | 2 (24) | 5 (60) | 4 (48) |
| Security posture | 10 | 5 (50) | 4 (40) | 3 (30) |
| Mobile experience | 8 | 3 (24) | 4 (32) | 4 (32) |
| AI features | 8 | 4 (32) | 2 (16) | 3 (24) |
| **Weighted total** | **100** | **381** | **376** | **330** |

Vendor A wins at 381, but notice what the matrix reveals that a gut call would have missed: Vendor A scores 2 on data portability, a 12-point criterion — the team is betting on a tool it can't easily leave. That's a risk to investigate during the trial, not a reason to discard the spreadsheet. The matrix's job isn't to remove judgment; it's to point judgment at the right questions.

One more discipline: any vendor scoring a 1 on a must-have criterion (security compliance, for example) is disqualified regardless of total score. Write this "killer criterion" rule into the rubric before scoring starts, so nobody has to make the uncomfortable call live. ## Finishing the Scorecard: Qualitative Notes, the Business Case, and Next Time

Not everything belongs in numbers. Interview impressions, demo chemistry, and documentation quality rarely survive translation into a 1–5 score, so give each candidate one "Notes" row at the bottom of the sheet, kept out of the weighted total. When totals land within three or four points of each other, that paragraph is the tiebreaker — written as observed behavior, not vibes: "Vendor B's support engineer debugged our SSO issue live during the trial" beats "Vendor B seemed friendly."

The final document should fit on one page: criteria and weights, the scored table, totals per vendor, and a three-line recommendation citing the criteria where the winner won. That page is your decision record, budget justification, and defense against second-guessing. Every evaluation also teaches you something about your own weights, so two habits compound across purchase cycles: archive every matrix with a short post-mortem ("we underestimated mobile usage"), and reuse the rubric for renewals, not just new purchases. The same scorecard that picked the tool can tell you a year later whether it deserves renewal — and gives you the documentation to negotiate with the incumbent if it doesn't.

**Actionable takeaways:**

1. Weights must sum to 100 and be negotiated before any vendor interaction.
2. Six to ten outcome-based criteria, never feature checklists.
3. Score the incumbent first — it calibrates everything and builds your business case.
4. Use written anchors for the 1–5 scale so scores mean the same thing to every rater.
5. Keep one killer criterion (security, compliance, data portability) that disqualifies on its own.
6. Package the result as a one-page decision record, and reuse the rubric at renewal time.
