# How Long Does Software Evaluation Really Take? A Data-Backed Speed-Up Playbook

Ask a SaaS buyer how long a purchase cycle takes, and you'll get an answer between "a few weeks" and "it depends." Ask them how long it should take, and you'll get silence. The uncomfortable reality is that most software evaluations run three to six months, and most of that time is not spent evaluating — it's spent waiting. Waiting for calendars to align, for demo scheduling, for stakeholders to respond to the document you sent them, and for the trial to end so the real work can begin.

The good news is that the delays are structural, not inherent. Software evaluation has a floor — you can't responsibly buy a system your team will use daily in two business days — but the typical cycle is 60–70% overhead. This playbook separates the work that actually needs the time from the waiting that doesn't, and lays out a two-week process that compresses the whole cycle without cutting corners.

## Why Evaluations Drag: The Four Time Sinks

Before the fix, the diagnosis. Four causes account for nearly all evaluation delay:

**Stakeholder alignment.** The buying group can't agree on what it needs. Each department has its own priorities, nobody has written them down, and every vendor meeting resets the conversation. This is the most expensive delay in the entire cycle, because it compounds: every downstream step waits on the definition that nobody finished. Evaluations that stall in month two are almost always stalled here.

**The demo treadmill.** The average B2B evaluation involves 3–5 vendor demos, each taking 1–2 hours of a team's time, spread over weeks of calendar friction. Worse, most demos are generic — the vendor shows its standard slides and your team watches politely, then needs another meeting to discuss whether what they saw matches what they needed (see sink one).

**Trial limbo.** The free trial period — typically 14 to 30 days — feels like a natural deadline, but trials don't close themselves. Without assigned tasks, a named trial champion, and a fixed review date, the trial dissolves into a second calendar negotiation. Trials also fail from both sides: the vendor's trial is either too shallow to test real workflows or so broad that nobody knows where to start.

**Internal approval.** Even a perfect evaluation hits the procurement queue, and procurement processes are built on waiting: RFI response times, legal review, security questionnaires. These gates have their own weeks, and they don't compress well — which means the evaluation itself needs to finish early enough to leave room for them.

## The Data on Cycle Lengths

The numbers on real-world cycles are consistent across survey data: enterprise purchases average four to six months from first contact to signature; mid-market purchases run two to four months; and small-business purchases run three to eight weeks. Two patterns repeat in every dataset:

First, the tail is long. A small minority of purchases exceed a year, and those are almost never complex technical purchases — they're stalled ones, where the evaluators kept "keeping options open" instead of deciding. Second, the cycle doesn't correlate with price. A $200-per-month tool can take longer to buy than a $200,000 platform, because the small purchase lacks process discipline — no owner, no deadline, no defined requirements.

This is the key insight: duration is a function of process structure, not purchase size. Teams with written requirements, a named decision owner, and a fixed deadline complete evaluations in a third of the time of teams without them, at the same decision quality. The two-week process below is built on exactly those three levers.

## The Two-Week Structure: What Actually Gets Done

Here's the compressed process, laid out day by day. It assumes a decision group of five to seven people, one owner, and one tool being evaluated.

**Week 1 — Definition and shortlist (Days 1–3).** Day one: the owner writes a one-page requirements document — three to five must-haves, three to five nice-to-haves, and a budget band. Day two: a single 60-minute meeting where the group edits the document and assigns weights to the must-haves. No scoring, no debate about tools yet — just the requirements. Day three: the owner builds a shortlist of four candidates from that document, not from vendor marketing. If you can't reduce 20 candidate tools to four in one day, your requirements document is too vague.

**Week 1 — Verification (Days 4–5).** Two half-days, not five demos. The first half-day: a structured demo with the top two candidates, each given your requirements document in advance and asked to show specific workflows against it. The second half-day: a hands-on trial of the two finalists — not a vendor-scheduled walkthrough. Real tasks, from your workflow, done by your people. This is where the trial limbo problem gets solved: the trial is a bounded exercise with a deliverable, not an open-ended account.

**Week 2 — Scoring and decision (Days 6–9).** Day six: each stakeholder scores both finalists against the weighted requirements, independently. Day seven: the group meets for 90 minutes to compare scores, resolve the disagreements that matter, and pick a winner or a tiebreaker exercise. Day eight: reference calls — two per finalist — and a check of the winner's contract terms against the budget band. Day nine: the decision is documented in a one-page memo: what was evaluated, how it scored, what was chosen, and what the risks are. If you want to check whether your shortlist would survive a more rigorous scoring pass, [PilotStack](https://www.pilotstack.online/methodology) publishes its five-dimension scoring methodology openly — a useful benchmark to run your own criteria against.

**Week 2 — Exit (Days 10–14).** Negotiation and signature, with the renewal caps and implementation terms you identified before purchase. This week also includes the internal notification: the team that will use the tool learns about the decision and the rollout plan before the purchase is announced by invoice.

## What Gets Cut — and What Doesn't

The compressed process cuts four things: the demo treadmill (5 meetings → 2), open-ended trials (30 days → 2 bounded half-days), requirement meetings (3+ sessions → 1), and the "keep options open" period where the shortlist sits untouched for a month. It does not cut the things that protect you: independent scoring, reference calls, and the two-finalist structure that forces a real choice.

The most important subtraction is psychological. An evaluation without a deadline acquires an open-endedness that inflates every other step — vendors slow their responses, stakeholders schedule demos further out, and the requirements document drifts. A public deadline (the two-week structure, or a date set by the CFO's budget cycle) compresses all of it, because every participant starts behaving as if the date is real.

## The Two Refinements That Save Another Week

Two habits separate fast evaluations from slow ones, and both cost nothing:

**Demo with your document, not theirs.** Send the requirements document to vendors before the demo, and open the meeting with "show us how you handle workflow X from section 2." Vendors who can't address your requirements in a demo are signaling that their product is generic — that signal is worth a meeting you didn't plan to have.

**Timebox the trial with a task list.** A trial that starts with "here's our login, let us know if you have questions" is a failed trial. A trial with ten written tasks and a 48-hour deadline produces actual evaluation data. It also lets you compare finalists honestly, because both attempted the same tasks under the same time budget.

## When Slow Is Correct

Compression has a limit, and three situations legitimately break the two-week frame: purchases above a threshold where procurement mandates competitive bidding (add two to four weeks for the formal process); tools that hold critical data where security review needs its own cycle (run that in parallel, not sequentially); and multi-system replacements where the evaluation is really a migration plan (the migration deserves its own project plan — just don't let it hide inside the purchase decision).

In every other case, the honest answer to "how long should this take?" is: two weeks of structured work, bracketed by procurement gates you can't control. Everything beyond that is waiting, and waiting is a choice.

**Actionable takeaways:**

1. Cycle length is a function of process structure, not purchase size — fix the process, not the calendar.
2. One-page requirements written in a day are the single highest-leverage step; everything else hangs off them.
3. Replace 3–5 generic demos with 2 structured demos run against your document.
4. Timebox trials: two half-days, ten written tasks, a fixed review date.
5. End with a one-page decision memo, not a folder of notes.
6. Run security and procurement review in parallel with evaluation, and leave room for their fixed gate times.
