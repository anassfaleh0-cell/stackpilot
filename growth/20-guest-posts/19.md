# The 30-Day Software Audit Calendar: A Month to Fix Your Stack

The average mid-size company runs somewhere between 100 and 200 distinct software subscriptions, and nobody can name all of them. Tools get bought for a single project and never cancelled, trials auto-convert to paid plans, departments duplicate each other's licenses, and the annual bill quietly becomes a line item nobody owns. Studies of software spending consistently find that 20 to 30 percent of license spend goes to underused or unused software — meaning a company spending $500,000 a year on SaaS is likely burning six figures on shelfware. The fix does not require an expensive consultant or a special procurement platform. It requires one person, twenty working days, and a schedule. This is a day-by-day calendar for a 30-day software audit that ends with a leaner stack, a renewal calendar, and a list of contracts worth renegotiating.

## Why a month is the right frame

An audit that drags on gets abandoned; an audit that is too rushed produces findings nobody trusts. Twenty working days is enough time to pull data, interview the people who actually use the tools, and negotiate — and short enough that the work stays focused. Budget the math honestly: if the audit finds and removes even 15 percent of a $400,000 stack, it generates $60,000 in annual savings from a single employee's month of work. The cost of not auditing is also compounding: every unused license that renews another year is a bill you chose to pay for a tool nobody opened.

## Week 1 (days 1–5): Inventory and mapping

The audit starts with money, not with tools. Day 1: export every software invoice and subscription from accounting and procurement. Include recurring credit card charges, not just purchase orders — that is where ghost subscriptions hide. Day 2: add the IT view. Pull software inventory from your SSO provider, MDM, and endpoint management tools, and match it against the billing list. Anything billing for but missing from inventory is either shadow IT or waste; anything installed but never billed is shadow IT. Day 3: talk to department heads, one 30-minute meeting each. Ask two questions: which tools does your team actually rely on daily, and which tools did someone buy for a project that ended? Day 4: classify every tool on the master list into four buckets — business-critical, nice-to-have, redundant, and unused — and note the owner of each tool, or the absence of an owner. Day 5: build the master inventory spreadsheet. Columns: vendor, contract start and renewal date, auto-renewal clause, annual cost, seats licensed, seats active, department, owner, bucket, and next action. This single spreadsheet is the deliverable that makes everything else possible.

## Week 2 (days 6–10): Usage analysis and duplicates

Now quantify reality. Days 6–7: pull usage reports from the tools themselves — admin consoles export login activity, active user counts, and feature usage for the last 90 days. Define an "active user" in writing before you look at the data (e.g., logged in at least once in the last 90 days) so the numbers stay defensible. Where a tool has no usable usage report, that is a finding in itself: a vendor that cannot tell you who uses the product is a vendor you cannot manage. Day 8: compute cost per active user for every tool and flag anything below 40 percent seat utilization. The per-seat benchmarks published across the 900-plus side-by-side comparisons on [PilotStack](https://www.pilotstack.online) give you a realistic reference for what tools in each category should cost per active user — when your number runs 50 percent above the category norm, the problem is the deal, not the tool. A concrete example of why this matters: a company running two $6,000-per-year analytics tools for 30 employees, each averaging 6 monthly users, is paying roughly $167 per active user per month for reporting that could be consolidated into one tool and free native dashboards. Day 9: hunt duplicates. The classic pairs are two tools covering one workflow — a spreadsheet and a database doing the same job, two video-conferencing vendors, two chat platforms, an expensive analytics suite nobody opens next to free native reporting. Day 10: interview the heavy users of each duplicated pair and determine the winner by workflow, not by feature count. The tool your team actually uses on its worst day wins, even if the other one has a prettier logo. At the end of week 2 you should have a short list of unused licenses to cancel, duplicate pairs with a designated winner, and a savings estimate to check against week 4's reality.

## Week 3 (days 11–15): Renewal triage and rationalization

Week 3 turns findings into decisions. Day 11: list every contract renewing in the next 90 days, sorted by annual value, with its renewal date and auto-renewal window. Day 12: prioritize: rank the renewal list by spend multiplied by urgency, so the $60,000 enterprise contract renewing next month sits above the $1,200 analytics tool renewing next quarter. Day 13: assign each contract a decision: renew as-is, renegotiate, replace, or kill. Be honest about switching costs here — a tool with deep integration debt may be worth renegotiating rather than replacing even if usage is mediocre. Day 14: validate the kill list. For each candidate, check data export options, dependent integrations, and who else in the company might quietly rely on it. Cancel only what you have verified. Day 15: present weeks 1–3 to leadership with one page of numbers: current spend, identified waste, the kill list, the renegotiation list, and the expected savings range. Approval at day 15 buys you the mandate to negotiate in week 4.

## Week 4 (days 16–20): Negotiation and consolidation

Days 16–17: action the renegotiation list. For each contract, send the vendor a requirements letter 60 to 90 days before renewal: right-sized seat counts based on your usage data, requested discount, price-increase cap, and exit terms. Gather at least two competitive quotes for the biggest renewals — a credible alternative quote is the strongest single lever in a renewal conversation. Day 18: cancel the verified kill list with written confirmation, and export data from anything you are retiring before you close the accounts. Day 19: consolidate. Vendors who hold several small contracts with you are often willing to trade a unified agreement and a real discount for committed multi-year spend — one $30,000 agreement is easier to discount than three $10,000 ones. Day 20: close the audit with documentation: total annual savings achieved and pending, the revised master inventory with one named owner per tool, a 12-month renewal calendar with negotiation dates set 90 days before every renewal, and a 90-day re-check date where you verify that cancellations actually processed and users migrated.

## What the output should look like

A successful month delivers a stack that is 10 to 20 percent cheaper, a single owner per tool, a renewal calendar nobody can forget, and — the part most audits miss — a standing process. Re-run a lightweight version quarterly: pull the billing export, check the renewal calendar, and flag anything new that appeared without an owner. The 30-day audit is the deep clean; the quarterly 2-day check is what keeps the stack from re-bloating.

## The bottom line

Software sprawl is not a technology problem; it is a scheduling problem. One month, one owner, and a day-by-day plan convert a stack that silently leaks money into a stack with known owners, verified usage, negotiated contracts, and a renewal calendar. The tools are already installed; the invoice data already exists. What was missing was the calendar.
