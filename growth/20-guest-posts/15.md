# Migration Costs: The Budget Line Everyone Forgets

Every software purchase proposal has the same shape: a subscription price, a payback period, and a confident assumption that the data will simply move. The subscription price is compared across vendors with spreadsheet discipline. Migration — the cost of getting your data out of the old system and into the new one — is ignored until after the contract is signed, when it stops being negotiable. That's backwards. Migration routinely costs more than the first year of the new tool's subscription, it's the budget line most likely to blow the project's total cost, and it's one of the few cost factors you can actually measure before you buy. This article breaks migration into its workstreams, puts realistic numbers on each, and shows how the total should change your vendor decision.

## What Migration Actually Involves: The Six Workstreams

Migration is not "move the files over." It's six distinct workstreams, each with its own failure mode:

1. **Export** — pulling data out of the legacy system in a usable format.
2. **Mapping** — translating the old system's fields, values, and structure into the new system's.
3. **Cleaning** — deduplicating records, fixing broken references, normalizing inconsistent values.
4. **Validation** — confirming that what arrived is what was sent, record counts and field-by-field samples.
5. **Cutover** — the downtime window and the freeze on the old system before it.
6. **Rollback** — the plan for going back if validation fails, which you will need roughly 40% of the time on a first migration.

Teams budget workstream one and discover the other five. The gap between a migration estimate and a migration bill is the difference between "export" and "migration."

## Data Export Formats: The Detail That Decides Everything

The first question to ask any vendor is not what the product does — it's what the export does. The answer determines the migration's shape.

Some products export full-fidelity archives: records with metadata, attachments, comments, history, and timestamps, in a documented format. Some export CSV with the comments left behind. Some export everything except the attachments, which is a small footnote on the pricing page and a two-week apology when you discover your five years of file history is stranded in the old system. And some — the ones you should refuse — charge for export or make it a professional services engagement, which is a business model, not a feature.

The test is cheap and it belongs in the first week of any trial: export a meaningful batch of your real data — records, comments, attachments, history — and inspect what comes out. Check that timestamps survived in the right timezone, that enum values map to something sensible, and that the export is complete in count. Thirty minutes of testing here answers a question that will otherwise cost you weeks during the actual migration.

## The Mapping Effort: Where Budgets Double

Mapping is where migration cost estimates die, because it's invisible until you're inside it. Every field in the old system must be matched to a field in the new system, and every value must be translated: the old system's "High/Medium/Low" priority might be the new system's "P0/P1/P2/P3"; the old custom field "Deal Stage" may have 14 values the new system represents differently; user-assignment fields need mapping when the old system's owner no longer exists.

The realistic arithmetic: with clean data, mapping and transformation run roughly 20–40 hours per 10,000 records. With dirty data — duplicates, inconsistent date formats, free-text enums like "High" and "high!" and "High Priority" all meaning the same thing — multiply by three. The dirty-data multiplier is the most consistent finding in migration projects: teams that believed their legacy system was "mostly clean" discover the exceptions they've been working around for years are scattered through 40,000 records. If you suspect dirty data, budget the multiplier up front, and test it — pull a 500-record sample and count the duplicates and inconsistencies before you commit to a plan.

## Downtime, Training, and Dual-Running

Three costs complete the picture, and all three are routinely underestimated.

**Downtime.** The cutover window is a weekend for a small system — 8 to 24 hours, plus the data freeze on the old system in the days before, during which nobody can update records. For a business whose team is actively entering data, the freeze is the most disruptive part of the whole migration, and it's the part no vendor quotes.

**Dual-running.** The standard cutover pattern runs both systems in parallel for four to eight weeks while users move over in waves. That means paying both subscriptions simultaneously, entering data twice for the overlap period, and reconciling the two systems until the old one is switched off. Dual-running is insurance, and like all insurance it's annoying — but the alternative, a big-bang cutover on a Monday morning, is how migrations become emergencies.

**Training.** Every user who switches systems loses between 40% and 70% of their pre-migration throughput for the first two to four weeks. For a 25-person team at a loaded cost of $50 per hour, that's 25 people × 20 hours × $50 — about $25,000 of productivity, on a tool that might invoice you $9,000 a year. Training is never a line item in the purchase proposal, and it is consistently the largest single component of migration cost.

## The Realistic Cost Model: A Worked Example

Take a concrete case: a 25-person company migrating 40,000 records from a legacy CRM to a new tool at $30 per user per month. Internal labor at a loaded $50 per hour, with the team's operations lead doing the bulk of the work:

| Workstream | Hours | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Export and format audit | 16 | $800 |
| Field mapping and transforms | 24 | $1,200 |
| Data cleaning (dirty-data multiplier) | 20 | $1,000 |
| Validation and reconciliation | 12 | $600 |
| Cutover weekend and freeze management | 16 | $800 |
| Rollback dry-run and plan | 8 | $400 |
| Dual-running: 8 weeks of double entry | 60 | $3,000 |
| Dual-running: both subscriptions for 8 weeks | — | $1,400 |
| Training: 25 users × 20 hours at 50% productivity | — | $25,000 |
| **Total** | | **~$34,200** |

The subscription is $9,000 for the first year. The migration is roughly 3.8 times that — and this is a modest example. Teams that skip the export audit, inherit a heavily customized legacy system, or run a longer dual-running period routinely land at five to eight times the first-year subscription. That ratio is why migration cost deserves to be a scored criterion in the vendor evaluation, not a footnote in the implementation plan.

## How Migration Cost Should Change Your Vendor Choice

Migration cost is not an act of God; it's a design decision the vendor made, and it's one of the most legible differences between vendors you can measure before signing.

Weight data portability explicitly in your scorecard — it's the criterion that separates a tool you own from a tool that owns you. Favor vendors with documented import/export formats, self-serve import wizards, and published migration runbooks; each one of those converts migration hours from custom engineering into checklist work. Favor vendors who include a migration allowance in the contract — a migration credit is one of the most common negotiated concessions, and it's worth more at signing than at year two, when the exit door is closed. And treat a vendor's export behavior as a strategic signal: a vendor that makes export easy and free is confident its product will keep you; a vendor that charges for export, requires professional services for it, or exports in proprietary formats is engineering your retention. The 30-minute export test in week one of your trial is the single cheapest due diligence you will run on any software purchase.

When you're comparing vendors, the pricing pages won't show any of this — which is why evaluation frameworks that force a consistent look across vendors are valuable. Independent review platforms that test tools hands-on and publish a fixed rubric, like [PilotStack's methodology](https://www.pilotstack.online/methodology), score portability and value on the same scale for every tool; your job is to add the migration numbers from your own export test to that baseline before you sign.

## Conclusion

The cheapest software is the one you can leave. Migration cost is a feature of every product — determined by export formats, mapping complexity, downtime windows, dual-running length, and training load — and it's measurable before you sign. Run the export test in week one, budget the dirty-data multiplier, price the training, weight data portability in the scorecard, and negotiate a migration credit into the contract. Do that, and the migration that follows is a checklist. Skip it, and the migration you discover in month three is the real price of the software you thought you were buying.
