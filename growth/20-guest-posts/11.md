# What Review Sites Don't Tell You (and How to Verify Software Claims Yourself)

A 4.8-star average with 2,000 reviews looks like due diligence. It isn't. That number was produced by a machine you don't control: a review platform's ranking algorithm, the vendors who feed it, and reviewers whose companies look nothing like yours. The gap between what a review page tells you and what the software will actually do for your team is large enough to hold a failed implementation. Here are the four structural limits of review sites, then a 90-minute verification sequence that answers the questions reviews can't.

## The Four Structural Limits of Review Sites

**Paid placement.** Many "best of" lists and featured badges are commercial products. Vendors buy placement in the same way they buy ads, and the platforms' disclosure practices vary from a subtle label to nothing at all. That doesn't mean the lists are fabricated — but the top three results on a comparison page are a mix of who is best and who paid, and you can rarely tell which is which.

**Astroturfing.** Fake reviews are cheap to produce and hard to stop. The FTC's 2023 rulemaking on fake reviews made undisclosed paid reviews legally risky for vendors, but enforcement is spotty and the review economy has adapted: incentivized reviews disclosed in fine print, reviews written by founders' friends, and review swaps between SaaS companies with overlapping audiences. A product with a flawless review page can still be mediocre in practice.

**Missing context.** Reviews are written by people whose company size, industry, and stack differ from yours. A five-star review from a 200-person marketing team praising "granular role-based permissions" is describing a feature that is administrative overhead for your 12-person team. A one-star review complaining about enterprise pricing tiers says nothing about whether the small-business tier is good.

**Stale data.** SaaS products change every month. A review written fourteen months ago is a review of a different product — before the pricing restructure, before the API rewrite, before the feature your whole decision depends on was added or removed. Most platforms show a review's date in small gray text, and most buyers never read it.

None of this means review sites are useless. It means they are a starting point, not a verdict.

## Read the Changelog Before You Read the Marketing Page

The changelog is the raw truth. Marketing pages describe the product the vendor wants to sell; changelogs describe the product that exists. Every vendor publishes one — GitHub releases, a "What's New" page, release notes in the docs — and almost nobody reads it.

Look at three things. Release cadence: does the product ship weekly, monthly, or quarterly? A tool that ships monthly is responding to its customers; a tool that ships twice a year is responding to its roadmap meetings. Fix mix: does the changelog show bug fixes alongside features, or only features? A vendor that never documents fixes is either not shipping them or not being honest about them. Breaking changes: how often do API or integration changes break existing behavior, and how far in advance are they announced? A changelog with a "breaking changes" section followed by a warning period is a vendor you can plan around; one that buries the breaking change at the bottom of a release note is a vendor you will fight in year two.

The changelog also verifies marketing claims. A vendor announces "SSO on all plans" at its conference; the changelog three months later shows SSO shipping only to enterprise. That discrepancy, caught in ten minutes, is worth more than any demo. Recording which version you tested and when is the same discipline behind [PilotStack's methodology](https://www.pilotstack.online/methodology): the platform's analyses document the version and testing window behind every review — exactly the metadata regular review pages omit.

## Test the Support Desk Before You Buy

Support quality is the most misrepresented dimension in software, because nobody reviews it until they need it — and by then they've already signed. You can test it in a day, for free.

Open a trial account and send two questions through the real support channel: one trivial ("How do I change my plan?") and one technical ("Does your API support bulk export of attachments?"). Then grade the experience on three axes. Response time: an answer in 4 hours versus 4 days tells you more than any support review. Answer quality: did they actually solve the problem, or did they send a link to a help article that doesn't quite fit? And tone: was the reply reading your question, or pattern-matching to a script?

Do this while you're a free trial user, not a customer. The gap between trial support and paid support is real, but it's far smaller than the gap between sales support and everything after. The demo rep is the best employee the vendor has; the support tier is the product's actual customer experience.

## Mine the Community: Where Real Complaints Live

Every product has a place where users talk without the vendor moderating: GitHub issues, community forums, subreddits, Stack Overflow. The complaints there never make it to review pages — the people who hit them are too busy working around the problem to write a review.

For developer tools, GitHub issues are a goldmine. Count the open issues with a "bug" label and read how maintainers respond. A repository with 400 open issues but regular triage and fast closes is healthy; one with 400 open issues from eighteen months ago and no maintainer comments since 2024 is telling you something about the product's future.

For SaaS tools, search the community forum for threads with "workaround" in the title. A missing feature is a roadmap item; a missing feature with twelve community threads of workarounds is a permanent hole. Then search "[product name] + terrible", "[product name] + bug", and "[product name] + leaving" and read what comes up. The same people who wrote glowing reviews on the review platform will describe their real failure experience in a forum thread — because in the forum, nobody is collecting their ratings.

## Cross-Check Documentation Against the Trial

Documentation is the vendor's contract with reality. It tells you what the product supports; the trial tells you what it supports comfortably. Cross-check three specific claims during your trial instead of taking either source's word.

First, the export claim: docs say "full data export" — actually export a meaningful batch of your real records and check whether attachments, comments, and history come along. Second, the integration claim: docs list a native integration — create the connection in the trial and watch for sync failures or field limits. Third, the scale claim: docs say "up to 50,000 records" — load a realistic dataset and time the operations that matter to you, like a filtered search or a report.

Keep a verification log: the claim, the doc reference, the version number, and the result of your test. That log becomes the appendix to your decision memo, and it is what you will re-read in month six when the vendor's roadmap presentation sounds better than the product feels.

## The 90-Minute Verification Sequence

Here's the whole playbook in the order it should run:

1. **Send the support test first (5 minutes).** Open a trial account and fire off the two-question support email. The clock starts now, so the response time measures itself by the time you finish the rest.
2. **Read the changelog (15 minutes).** Scan the last six months of releases for cadence, fix mix, and breaking changes. Cross-check one marketing claim against it.
3. **Search the community (20 minutes).** GitHub issues, forum "workaround" threads, and the three search strings above. Note the top recurring complaints.
4. **Check the review page structurally (15 minutes).** Sort by most recent, count how many of the last ten reviews contain specific details versus generic praise, and check the date distribution. Great reviews from 14 months ago and silence since is a trajectory signal.
5. **Read the docs on your three critical workflows (25 minutes).** Find what the documentation says about the things your team does daily, and flag anything that requires "custom development" or "contact support."
6. **Grade the support response (10 minutes).** By now the support email has an answer. Time it, grade the quality, and record both.

## Conclusion

Review sites are a filter, not a verdict. They tell you which products deserve your attention, and their numbers are shaped by incentives that have nothing to do with your use case — paid placement, astroturfing, missing context, and stale data. The 90-minute sequence costs less than one afternoon and answers the questions no review page can: Does the product actually do what the changelog says it does? Does the vendor support its customers or just sell to them? Can you get your data back out? Verify the claims yourself, and the review pages become what they were always meant to be — a starting point you've already left behind.
