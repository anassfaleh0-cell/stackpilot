# How to Write a Software Requirements Document (RFP) That Vendors Take Seriously

An RFP is a first date, and most RFPs show up unprepared. Vendors receive requests that list thirty features without explaining why the buyer needs any of them, attach no business context, demand responses in ten business days, and include scoring criteria that appear to have been invented after the shortlist. Vendors respond to those documents the way anyone responds to a date with a script: politely, generically, and without committing to anything.

A serious RFP changes the vendor's behavior, because it gives them the two things they actually need to write a real response: your constraints and your decision logic. When vendors understand the business problem, the acceptance criteria, and the scoring weights, they respond with specifics instead of marketing language. Here is how to write that document.

## Start with Business Context, Not Features

The most common RFP mistake is opening with a feature list. Features without context are noise — a vendor cannot tell you whether their "reporting" meets your needs if you have not said what the report is for. Start instead with a business context section that answers five questions in plain language: what problem is being solved, for which teams and how many users, at what volumes (records, tickets, transactions per month), what the current system fails at, and what the deadline is and why.

Volume is the most valuable line in this section. "We process 40,000 support tickets a year with a team of twelve" tells a vendor more about the right architecture than a page of features ever will. Deadline context matters too: if the decision must land by Q3 because the current contract expires in November, say so. Vendors staff their bid teams around deadlines, and an honest one gets prioritized over an ambiguous one. A concrete example: two identical RFPs for a CRM reached vendors in the same week; one said the buying team had fifteen salespeople, 6,000 opportunities a year, and a renewal date; the other listed 40 features. The first vendor built a response around data quality, migration, and reporting — things that mattered — while the second vendor guessed, and guessed wrong.

## Separate Must-Haves from Nice-to-Haves

With context in place, list requirements in two buckets. Must-haves are gate criteria: if the product fails one, the vendor is excluded, no score. Nice-to-haves are differentiators that get scored and weighted. The discipline of the split is what forces you to think about what you cannot live without — and the limit is important: no more than ten must-haves, because every must-have you add shrinks the qualified vendor pool and raises the price of the eventual winner.

Write every requirement as a testable statement, not a product name. "Must support SSO" is untestable; "must support SAML 2.0 SSO with the identity provider in use at our company" is a test a vendor can pass or fail on day one of the trial. Replace "intuitive interface" — which means nothing and is impossible to score — with a defined test: "a non-technical user completes the core workflow in under 30 minutes with no training." Vendors respect requirements they can verify, and they respond accordingly.

## Define the Evaluation Criteria and Scoring Weights

The single strongest signal that an RFP is serious is published scoring weights. Typical weights for a mid-market evaluation: functional fit 35 percent, total cost of ownership 20 percent, implementation and migration effort 15 percent, vendor stability and support 15 percent, security and compliance 10 percent, and contract terms 5 percent. Publish the weights in the RFP, define the scoring scale (for example, 1–5 against the acceptance criteria, with a written justification for every score below 3), and commit to the scoring model before responses arrive.

Published weights do three things. They tell vendors where to spend their response effort — a vendor who knows functional fit is 35 percent and contract terms 5 percent writes a different proposal. They prevent post-hoc score rearranging when the CFO falls in love with a cheaper option. And they give the selection committee a defensible decision trail. This is the same discipline independent evaluation teams apply: fixed dimensions, published weights, and scores that must be justified — the approach [PilotStack's reviewers](https://www.pilotstack.online/methodology) use when scoring 150+ tools, and it works for a single purchase for exactly the same reason.

## Set Realistic Timelines

Unrealistic timelines are how RFPs self-sabotage. A realistic schedule for a mid-market purchase looks like this: RFP released on day 1, questions due day 10, vendor responses due day 24, response scoring and shortlist day 24–31, scripted trials and reference calls days 31–45, final decision and negotiation days 45–52, contract signed by day 60. Compress any stage and you will get one of two things: responses from vendors who have no chance of winning but free capacity, or partial responses from the vendors you actually want. A ten-day response window does not filter out unserious vendors; it filters out serious vendors, who would rather decline than submit an unusable bid to a buyer who has already shown they do not plan ahead.

Build in one buffer: a "questions and clarifications" period is mandatory, because the vendors who ask good clarifying questions are the vendors you want to meet, and the vendor who sends a 40-page response without a single question has not read your business context. Ask for responses in a format, not freeform: fixed sections mirroring your scoring weights, with hard limits on page count. The RFP is a test of discipline, and the response format is part of the test.

## The Common RFP Mistakes to Avoid

Five mistakes kill more RFPs than any vendor failure. First, feature-name requirements without acceptance criteria — the vendor can claim anything, so they do. Second, no published weights — the decision looks arbitrary, and the losing vendors complain loudly, sometimes to your leadership. Third, evaluating vendors on responses alone, without a scripted trial or demo of the must-haves. Fourth, ignoring total cost of ownership — evaluating on license price while ignoring migration, integration, and admin effort, then discovering the "cheap" vendor costs twice as much in year two. Fifth, scoring after the fact — collecting scores from committee members who never attended the trials or read the responses, then averaging opinions instead of evidence.

Two more errors deserve mention because they are invisible until the room is full. Asking for every feature the incumbent has — an RFP that is secretly a renewal decision will be treated as theater by every vendor smart enough to notice, and as a real deal by exactly the vendor you should not pick. And inviting too many vendors: more than five bidders halves the average response quality, because bid teams split their best people across the opportunities most likely to close. Keep the bidders at three to five, keep the must-haves testable, and keep the scoring in the document.

Each mistake has a fix, and the fix is always the same: write the requirement testably, publish the weights, run the trials, price the total cost, and collect scores only from people who attended the evidence.

## Conclusion

Vendors take RFPs seriously when the document takes itself seriously. Business context with real volumes, ten testable must-haves, published scoring weights, a timeline with a questions period, and a fixed response format — those five elements change the responses you receive more than any template ever will. Write the document the way you would want to receive it: specific, scored, and evidence-based. The vendors who respond well to that document are exactly the vendors you should be choosing from.
